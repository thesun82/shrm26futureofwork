Section 03

Human Work Audit™

A six-step process to observe, map, categorize, and redesign work for intentional human–AI collaboration. Use this with your team, your department, or a single role you want to rethink.

1
Step 01
Observe the Work
Watch what people actually do — not what the job description says. What problems do they solve? Where do they use judgment? What does "good" look like in practice?
  • Shadow employees during a typical workday (with their consent)
  • Ask: "Walk me through the last time something unexpected happened. What did you do?"
  • Note the moments when they pause, adapt, consult someone, or improvise
  • Distinguish what gets written down from what actually gets done
The job description is the map. Observation is the territory.
2
Step 02
Map Hidden Labor
Much of the most valuable work is invisible in dashboards and job descriptions. Name it explicitly before it gets automated away.
  • Emotional labor: managing relationships, tone, and team climate
  • Trust-building: the informal connections that make collaboration possible
  • Escalation handling: knowing when, how, and to whom to escalate
  • Exception management: the cases that don't fit any process
  • Institutional memory: knowing what was tried, and why it failed
This work is invisible in the data. It is critical in practice.
3
Step 03
Categorize the Work
For each responsibility, ask: Is this Task Work? Is this Judgment Work? Is this Human Work? Clarity here changes everything about how you design the role.
  • Task Work: Repeatable, rules-based, predictable → AI-led with human review
  • Judgment Work: Ambiguous, contextual, involves tradeoffs → human-led with AI support
  • Human Work: Relational, cultural, trust-based → human-owned, full stop
Use the Reflection Tool in Section 04 to do this for your own role right now.
4
Step 04
Redesign the Role
Move humans toward the work that only humans can do. Move AI toward structure and repetition. A role redesigned around human capability is not a diminished role — it is an elevated one.
  • Reduce time spent on repetitive, low-judgment tasks
  • Protect time for relationship-building, coaching, and strategic thinking
  • Ask: If AI handled all the Task Work, what would this person be freed to do?
  • Shift job descriptions to reflect actual human value — judgment, relationships, leadership
The goal is not fewer humans. It is better-deployed humans.
5
Step 05
Deploy AI Intentionally
Automate friction. Not people. AI should reduce cognitive load so humans can do more human work — not replace the judgment, relationships, and meaning-making that make organizations function.
  • Identify specific tasks where AI adds value without introducing risk
  • Maintain human review on anything involving judgment, ethics, or relationships
  • Ask: Does this deployment preserve human intelligence — or quietly erode it?
  • Design AI as an amplifier of human capability, not a substitute for human presence
AI handles the tasks. Humans handle the meaning.
6
Step 06
Monitor for Drift
Is the system still solving the right problem? Or has it become the Car Wash Problem again — optimizing for the wrong variable? Revisit. Recalibrate. Stay intentional.
  • Schedule regular check-ins: Are humans still doing the work that matters most?
  • Watch for over-automation: Are shortcuts quietly eroding judgment capacity?
  • Ask: What would tell us AI is now solving the wrong problem?
  • Recalibrate whenever roles, team structures, or organizational needs shift
"If the car wash is 30 feet from my house, should I take my car there?" The answer depends entirely on what you're actually trying to solve for.

Section 05

The better questions to ask

The question you start with shapes the solution you find. How you frame AI and work determines what you build — and what you accidentally break.

The wrong question
"How many humans can AI replace?"
→
The better question
"What kind of work is this?"
The wrong question
"Can AI do this job?"
→
The better question
"What does this role require that AI cannot provide?"
The wrong question
"How do we do more with less?"
→
The better question
"What is the human's role when the machine can do the task?"
Applying the framework: questions by work type
Task Work
"Can we automate this without losing quality or human oversight?"
If it's repeatable and rules-based, AI-led is appropriate — with human review built in.
Judgment Work
"What context does a human need to lead this decision well?"
AI can synthesize information. Humans must weigh the tradeoffs and own the consequences.
Human Work
"What would be lost if we removed the human from this interaction?"
If the answer is trust, care, or cultural meaning — keep the human at the center.
🌱
The technology should remove cognitive friction. Preserve human intelligence. Amplify human capability. AI handles the tasks. Humans handle the meaning.

Bonus · Outfit Explanation

The Cthulhu Problem

H.P. Lovecraft's Cthulhu is ancient, incomprehensible, and impossibly powerful. When it appeared, humans split into three camps — and nobody stopped to actually understand what it was. Sound familiar?

H.P. Lovecraft, 1926
Ancient. Incomprehensible. Impossibly powerful.
When Cthulhu appeared, no one stopped to actually understand what it was. They just reacted. And the reaction told you everything about the reactor — not the entity.
🙏
Worship It
Adopt AI wholesale. Trust it completely. Remove humans. Let it make the decisions. Build an entire strategy around something you don't actually understand. Efficiency is the new god.
😱
Panic
Freeze. Ban it. Fear displacement above all else. Refuse to engage. Frame every development as a threat. The sky is falling. The jobs are gone. We're doomed.
🙈
Pretend It Isn't Happening
Use it quietly but never address it organizationally. Avoid the conversation. Issue a one-paragraph "AI policy" and call it done. We'll deal with it later.
👀
Nobody stopped to actually understand what it was.
…sound familiar?
The Fourth Option
Actually understand what it is — and what it isn't.
Generative AI is a language system. It learned the broadcast of human intelligence — not the signal. It is extraordinarily capable at Task Work. It cannot reliably perform Judgment Work. It should never own Human Work. That distinction is not a talking point. It is the architecture of everything that comes next for HR and organizational design.

Bonus · The Reveal

Why You Were Staring

If you were in the room today, you noticed the outfit before you heard a word. That was the point.

Pattern Interruption

The brain is a prediction machine. It builds models of familiar situations to conserve energy — you walk into a conference session, and your nervous system already knows what to expect. Disrupting that groove demands full presence before a single slide appears. Novelty is one of the few reliable triggers for involuntary attention.

The Real Estate Argument

You were going to be looking at me for an hour. That's significant visual real estate, and I wasn't going to waste it. A question left open keeps the mind engaged. Telling you I'd explain it at the end guaranteed you'd stay curious — and present — for the whole session. The outfit was the open loop.

What You Actually Experienced — Before Slide One

01
Involuntary attention capture — novelty response triggered before conscious thought
02
Mild cognitive dissonance — the brain flagged something that didn't compute and demanded resolution
03
Suspended resolution — the open loop kept you engaged and present for the full session
04
Metacognitive awareness — you were invited to observe your own thinking in real time
All of that happened before slide one.

The Business Card

Try it now

Section 06

Works cited & research grounding

This framework is grounded in foundational research across organizational sensemaking, emotional intelligence, tacit knowledge, naturalistic decision-making, and psychological safety. Citations anchor the theory beneath the vivid delivery.

  • 1Weick, K. E. (1995). Sensemaking in Organizations. Sage Publications. (Foundational theory on how humans make meaning from ambiguous organizational experience — the basis for Narrative Sensemaking as a uniquely human capability.)
  • 2Goleman, D. (1995). Emotional Intelligence: Why It Can Matter More Than IQ. Bantam Books. (The foundational case for emotional intelligence as a distinct form of human capability — relevant to both Psychological Insight and Relational Intelligence.)
  • 3Edmondson, A. C. (2019). The Fearless Organization: Creating Psychological Safety in the Workplace for Learning, Innovation, and Growth. Wiley. (Psychological safety as the relational and cultural substrate that enables genuine organizational intelligence — a Human Work competency.)
  • 4Polanyi, M. (1966). The Tacit Dimension. University of Chicago Press. ("We can know more than we can tell." The philosophical foundation for tacit knowledge and why human expertise cannot be fully captured in language, data, or training sets.)
  • 5Klein, G. (1998). Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions. MIT Press. (Naturalistic decision-making — how experienced humans use pattern recognition, mental simulation, and intuition to make high-stakes decisions that no algorithm can replicate.)
  • 6Edmondson, A. C. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. Administrative Science Quarterly, 44(2), 350–383. (The foundational empirical study establishing psychological safety as a team-level variable.)
  • 7Dreyfus, H. L., & Dreyfus, S. E. (1986). Mind Over Machine: The Power of Human Intuition and Expertise in the Era of the Computer. Free Press. (A critique of rule-based AI and why expert human judgment requires situational understanding that cannot be reduced to explicit procedures.)
  • 8Simons, D. J., & Chabris, C. F. (1999). Gorillas in our midst: Sustained inattentional blindness for dynamic events. Perception, 28(9), 1059–1074. (The Reveal — involuntary attention and cognitive novelty response.)
  • 9Berlyne, D. E. (1960). Conflict, Arousal, and Curiosity. McGraw-Hill. (Novelty, arousal, and the sustaining of attention — the science behind pattern interruption.)
  • 10Corbetta, M., & Shulman, G. L. (2002). Control of goal-directed and stimulus-driven attention in the brain. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 3, 201–215. (Neural basis of bottom-up vs. top-down attention — foundational to The Reveal.)
  • 11Porges, S. W. (2011). The Polyvagal Theory: Neurophysiological Foundations of Emotions, Attachment, Communication, and Self-Regulation. Norton. (Autonomic nervous system states, social engagement system, and safety cues — the science behind the business card design.)
